Implied Probability and Margin: Finding the Fair Price

By the BetNow.tv Analytics Desk · 6 min read · Updated 2026-11-16

Implied probability is the chance of an outcome according to a price. Margin is the extra percentage built into a full market. Together they let you translate any line into a clean, fair forecast you can compare with a model.

Step 1 — convert each outcome

Take a soccer match priced at Home 2.10, Draw 3.40, Away 3.60. Implied probabilities are 1/2.10 = 47.6%, 1/3.40 = 29.4%, 1/3.60 = 27.8%.

Step 2 — add them up

47.6 + 29.4 + 27.8 = 104.8%. The margin is the amount above 100%: 4.8%. The "payout rate" is 100 ÷ 104.8 = 95.4%, meaning that in the long run only 95.4% of money staked on this market would be returned across all outcomes.

Step 3 — normalise to remove the margin

The simplest approach (proportional normalisation) divides each implied probability by the total:

  • Home: 47.6 ÷ 1.048 = 45.4%
  • Draw: 29.4 ÷ 1.048 = 28.1%
  • Away: 27.8 ÷ 1.048 = 26.5%

These now sum to 100% and represent the market's fair view.

Try it yourself with the margin calculator — enter two or three prices and see the margin and fair probabilities update live.

Better normalisation methods

Proportional normalisation assumes the margin is spread evenly. In reality, longshots often carry more of it — the well-documented favourite–longshot bias. Analysts therefore use alternatives such as the "power" method (raising probabilities to an exponent until they sum to 1) or Shin's method, which models a share of informed money. For two-outcome markets the differences are small; for multi-runner markets (golf, racing, outrights) they can be large.

Why this matters for prediction

A prediction model is only interesting relative to a benchmark. If our model says the home side wins 52% and the fair market says 45%, that seven-point gap is a hypothesis worth investigating: is the model missing an injury, or is the market overreacting to a recent loss? Learning to compute fair probability is the first step in thinking like an analyst rather than a fan.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing a model to raw (margin-included) probabilities — this makes every outcome look overpriced.
  • Ignoring the draw in soccer — two-way thinking in a three-way sport distorts every number.
  • Treating a single price as truth — prices move as information arrives.
