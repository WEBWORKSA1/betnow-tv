How Sports Odds Work: Decimal, American and Fractional Explained

By the BetNow.tv Analytics Desk · 7 min read · Updated 2026-11-16

Every sports price is a probability wearing a costume. Once you can see through the costume, previews, pundit takes and prediction models all become easier to judge. This guide walks through the three formats you will meet — decimal, American and fractional — and shows how each one converts into the only number that matters: the chance of an outcome happening.

Decimal odds

Decimal odds are common in Europe, Canada, Australia and India. The number is the total return per 1 unit staked, including the stake itself. Odds of 2.50 mean a 1-unit stake returns 2.50 units (1.50 profit plus the original 1). The implied probability is simply 1 ÷ decimal odds: 1 ÷ 2.50 = 0.40, or 40%.

That relationship is why analysts like decimal odds: they map onto probability with a single division. A favourite at 1.25 implies 80%; an outsider at 6.00 implies about 16.7%.

American (moneyline) odds

American odds pivot around 100. A positive number shows the profit on a 100 stake: +150 means 150 profit on 100. A negative number shows how much you must stake to make 100 profit: −200 means staking 200 to win 100.

  • Positive odds → probability = 100 ÷ (odds + 100). For +150: 100 ÷ 250 = 40%.
  • Negative odds → probability = |odds| ÷ (|odds| + 100). For −200: 200 ÷ 300 = 66.7%.

Note that +150 and 2.50 decimal describe exactly the same price — 40%.

Fractional odds

Traditional in the UK and Ireland, fractional odds show profit relative to stake. 3/2 means 3 profit for every 2 staked. Convert to decimal by dividing and adding one: 3 ÷ 2 + 1 = 2.50. Again: 40%.

Shortcut: convert anything to decimal first, then take 1 ÷ decimal to get probability. Our free odds converter does this instantly.

Why the probabilities add up to more than 100%

If you convert every outcome of a market — home, draw, away — and add the implied probabilities, you will usually get something like 104–108%. The excess is the bookmaker's margin (also called overround or "vig"). It is how a price-maker earns regardless of the result. Removing it gives the fair probabilities, which is what we compare our models against. We explain the method in Implied Probability and Margin.

Odds are opinions, not facts

A price is a market's estimate, shaped by information, public sentiment and risk management. It can be wrong. That is exactly why independent, transparent models are interesting: they let you compare a data-driven estimate to the crowd's estimate and ask why they differ — injuries, schedule fatigue, travel, weather or simple popularity bias.

Key takeaways

  • All three formats describe the same thing: probability.
  • Decimal → probability is 1 ÷ odds.
  • Market probabilities include a margin; strip it out for a fair view.
