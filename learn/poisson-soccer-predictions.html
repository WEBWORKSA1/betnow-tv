How Poisson Models Predict Soccer Scores

By the BetNow.tv Analytics Desk · 8 min read · Updated 2026-11-16

Goals in soccer are rare, roughly independent events — which makes them a natural fit for the Poisson distribution. With just two numbers, each team's expected goals, a Poisson model produces a probability for every possible scoreline, and from those, win/draw/loss and over/under figures.

Step 1 — estimate expected goals (λ)

A simple approach uses attack and defence strength relative to league average:

  • Home attack strength = home team's home goals per game ÷ league average home goals.
  • Away defence strength = away team's goals conceded away ÷ league average away goals conceded.
  • Home λ = home attack × away defence × league average home goals.

Repeat in reverse for the away side. Modern analysts often replace raw goals with xG (expected goals from shot quality), which is more stable over small samples.

Step 2 — scoreline probabilities

The probability a team scores exactly k goals is e−λ λk ÷ k!. Multiply the home and away probabilities for each pair to get a grid of scorelines — 0–0, 1–0, 1–1 and so on.

Step 3 — aggregate

Sum cells where home goals exceed away goals for a home win, the diagonal for a draw, and the rest for an away win. Sum cells where total goals exceed 2 for "over 2.5". Our Poisson predictor does all of this live.

Worked example: Home λ = 1.6, Away λ = 1.1 gives roughly Home 49%, Draw 25%, Away 26%, with 1–1 the single likeliest score and 1–0 close behind.

Known weaknesses

  • Draws are underestimated. Low scores are slightly correlated (game states, tactics). The Dixon–Coles adjustment (1997) corrects low-score cells.
  • Team strength changes. Weighting recent matches more heavily (time decay) helps.
  • Context is missing. Injuries, rotation, motivation and weather are not in the raw numbers.

Why it is still useful

Poisson models are transparent, fast and surprisingly competitive as a baseline. They are a superb learning tool: every assumption is visible, so you can see exactly why a model disagrees with consensus.
